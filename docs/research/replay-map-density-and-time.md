# Contour the deaths, ring the fights by time

Draw the death map as a **kernel density estimate computed on the native 150x150 game grid, smoothed with a Gaussian of sigma 6–8 cells (or a quartic kernel of radius ~20 cells), cut into 5 nested classes with a floor a few percent above zero, filled with a light-to-dark sequential ramp at ~0.5 group opacity and outlined with a 0.5–1 px contour stroke**; draw the engagements as **translucent discs (fill-opacity 0.25–0.4) with a 1.25 px ring in a sequential time color, radius proportional to the square root of the fight's size, painted in chronological order so the newest sits on top, numbered, and keyed by a horizontal time bar plus a one-dimensional timeline strip**. Every mainstream density tool (d3-contour, QGIS, ArcGIS, Leaflet.heat, Datashader) does the same bin-blur-color pipeline, and its defaults translate to roughly these numbers at 3 px per map cell; cartography settles on 3–7 classes with 5 as the default and 7 as the ceiling; the color literature is unanimous that ordered data (density, time) takes a monotonic-lightness ramp and never a rainbow. The two layers share a pitfall list: stacked semi-transparent fills produce colors that are not in the legend, so put the opacity on the group (density) or on the ring plus draw order (engagements), not on each overlapping fill. Marching squares with linear edge interpolation is a 200-line job in Rust and gives smooth isolines; a classed `<rect>` mosaic is the 20-line fallback that reads just as well at 28rem.

## Smooth deaths with a 6–8 cell kernel on the game's own grid

The density layer is a solved problem with a shared recipe: bin the points onto a grid, convolve with a radially symmetric kernel, then contour or colorize the grid. The kernel shape barely matters and the bandwidth is everything. The multivariate KDE literature calls the bandwidth "the single most important factor affecting its accuracy" while the kernel choice "is not crucial to the accuracy" ([Wikipedia, Multivariate KDE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_kernel_density_estimation)); ArcGIS and QGIS use a quartic kernel after Silverman (1986, eq. 4.5) ([ArcGIS Pro, How Kernel Density works](https://doc.esri.com/en/arcgis-pro/latest/tool-reference/spatial-analyst/how-kernel-density-works.html); [QgsHeatmapRenderer source](https://api.qgis.org/api/3.40/qgsheatmaprenderer_8cpp_source.html)), d3 uses a Gaussian ([d3-contour density docs](https://d3js.org/d3-contour/density)), and Leaflet.heat stamps a blurred disc ([simpleheat.js](https://unpkg.com/simpleheat@0.4.0/simpleheat.js)). Pick whichever is easier to write; a quartic kernel `w = (1 - (d/R)^2)^2` for `d < R` has compact support and needs no padding, and over 22,500 cells with R = 20 it costs about 28 million multiply-adds, which is nothing.

The defaults of the tools converge once translated to the target. d3's default bandwidth is **sigma = 20.49 px on a 960x500 canvas with a 4 px cell**, and the odd value is just the bandwidth whose blur radius is the integer 20 ([d3-contour src/density.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/d3/d3-contour/main/src/density.js)); at 28rem (448 px for 150 cells, so ~3 px per cell) that is **sigma ≈ 7 map cells and a cell of ≈ 1.3 map cells**. ArcGIS's default cell size is "the shorter of the width or height of the extent divided by 250" ([ArcGIS Pro, Kernel Density](https://doc.esri.com/en/arcgis-pro/latest/tool-reference/spatial-analyst/kernel-density.html)), i.e. 0.6 game cells, finer than the game grid itself. Leaflet.heat's radius 25 px plus blur 15 px ([Leaflet.heat README](https://github.com/Leaflet/Leaflet.heat)) lands in the same range. So **compute on the native 150x150 grid** (or a 75x75 grid of 2x2 blocks if DOM weight matters later) and start with **sigma = 7 cells**; because d3 pads its grid by 3 sigma ([d3-contour src/density.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/d3/d3-contour/main/src/density.js)), a quartic radius of **R ≈ 3 sigma ≈ 20 cells** gives a visually comparable footprint.

The statistical rules of thumb say larger, and that is the one place to deviate from them knowingly. For two dimensions Scott's and Silverman's rules coincide at `h = n^(-1/6) * sigma` ([Wikipedia, Multivariate KDE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_kernel_density_estimation)); for n = 300 deaths that factor is 0.386, so deaths spread over most of the map (sigma 35–40 cells) give h ≈ 13–15 cells and deaths clustered at a few battle sites (sigma ≈ 20) give h ≈ 8. But those rules assume one unimodal blob and are documented to oversmooth bimodal data ([Wikipedia, KDE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_density_estimation)); a game with fights at four bases is the textbook multimodal case, which is why ArcGIS had to adapt Silverman's rule to avoid "the ring around the points phenomenon" on sparse data ([ArcGIS Pro, How Kernel Density works](https://doc.esri.com/en/arcgis-pro/latest/tool-reference/spatial-analyst/how-kernel-density-works.html)). Keep **sigma in a 5–12 cell range, default 7**, and expose it as a slider if the user wants to tune it; below 5 cells individual deaths show as bumps, above 12 separate battles merge.

Bin with bilinear weights the way d3 does (`values[...] += (1 - xt) * (1 - yt) * wi`) ([d3-contour src/density.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/d3/d3-contour/main/src/density.js)) so a death at a cell boundary does not snap, and optionally weight each death by supply or mineral value, as QGIS and ArcGIS both allow with a weight field ([QGIS interpolation algorithms](https://docs.qgis.org/latest/en/docs/user_manual/processing_algs/qgis/interpolation.html)). Scale the result either as a density in a round unit (ArcGIS offers "Densities" per unit area and "Expected counts" per cell, "Count = Density x Area" ([ArcGIS Pro, Kernel Density](https://doc.esri.com/en/arcgis-pro/latest/tool-reference/spatial-analyst/kernel-density.html))) or relative to the grid maximum, which is what QGIS's renderer (`pixVal = min(value / scaleMax, 1)`) and Leaflet.heat (`max` = 1) do ([QgsHeatmapRenderer source](https://api.qgis.org/api/3.40/qgsheatmaprenderer_8cpp_source.html); [Leaflet.heat README](https://github.com/Leaflet/Leaflet.heat)). "Deaths per 10x10 cells" is the honest absolute unit; "% of peak" is the honest relative one.

## Five nested bands, light to dark, one group opacity

Cartography gives a narrow answer on class count: "If you want to be safe, make a map with 3–7 data classes" ([Axis Maps, Choropleth guide](https://www.axismaps.com/guide/choropleth)), and "a range of seven colors is the approximate upper limit of colors that can be clearly distinguished on a map" ([ArcGIS Pro, Graduated colors](https://doc.esri.com/en/arcgis-pro/latest/help/mapping/layer-properties/graduated-colors.html)). Use **5 classes** (six thresholds counting the unpainted background) and never more than 7. d3's density generator produces "about twenty nicely-rounded density thresholds" by default ([d3-contour density docs](https://d3js.org/d3-contour/density)), which is right for a smooth-looking stack and wrong for a legend-readable map; pass explicit breaks instead.

Choose the breaks for a right-skewed surface. Most cells are near zero and a handful of battle hotspots carry the maximum, which is exactly the case where Axis Maps says to avoid equal intervals ("if your data are skewed to one end or if you have one or two really large outlier values") ([Axis Maps, Choropleth guide](https://www.axismaps.com/guide/choropleth)). Quantiles over the non-zero cells, geometric (log-spaced) breaks, or Datashader-style histogram equalization, which is Datashader's default transfer function (`how='eq_hist'`) ([Datashader API](https://datashader.org/api.html)), all give every class visible area. Keep the lowest threshold above zero; d3 says "the first value x0 should typically be greater than zero" ([d3-contour density docs](https://d3js.org/d3-contour/density)), and a **floor at roughly 5% of the peak density** keeps open ground unpainted (Leaflet.heat's default gradient only starts coloring at 0.4 of max, Datashader's `min_alpha` is 40/255 ([simpleheat.js](https://unpkg.com/simpleheat@0.4.0/simpleheat.js); [Datashader API](https://datashader.org/api.html))). If the app will compare games or time windows side by side, fix one break set across them: ArcGIS's Constant-vs-Dynamic distinction exists because viewers otherwise compare maps whose colors mean different densities ([ArcGIS Pro, Heat map symbology](https://doc.esri.com/en/arcgis-pro/latest/help/mapping/layer-properties/heat-map.html)), and Axis Maps says a map series needs "a single scheme that can be applied across all of the maps" ([Axis Maps, Choropleth guide](https://www.axismaps.com/guide/choropleth)).

The "topographic" reading comes from the nested bands, not from the hues, and the real hypsometric palette is the wrong choice here. The green-yellow-brown-grey-white convention is recognizably terrain ([Wikipedia, Hypsometric tints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypsometric_tints)), but a user study found it "accurately conveyed elevation but also suggested inaccurate vegetation, rainfall, and temperature information" ([Patterson & Jenny](https://cartographicperspectives.org/index.php/journal/article/download/cp75-patterson-jenny/html?inline=1)), and over a minimap that already uses terrain greens and browns the collision is total. Follow the sequential rule instead, "light colors for low data values" and dark for high ([ColorBrewer](https://colorbrewer2.org/learnmore/schemes_full.html)), with either a **single-hue light-to-dark ramp** in a hue the minimap does not use, or **viridis/cividis**: viridis is perceptually uniform and "relatively friendly to common forms of colorblindness" ([BIDS colormap](https://bids.github.io/colormap/)) and cividis gives "nearly-identical visual-data interpretation" to readers with and without color-vision deficiency ([Nuñez et al. 2018, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0199239)). Do not ship the Leaflet.heat default `blue -> cyan -> lime -> yellow -> red` ([simpleheat.js](https://unpkg.com/simpleheat@0.4.0/simpleheat.js)): it is the rainbow that "adds artificial boundaries to some parts of the data range, hiding small-scale variations elsewhere" ([Crameri et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7595127/)), and its lime band disappears against green terrain. When sampling a dark-ending ramp over a dark minimap, take the 5 class colors from the **[0.15, 1.0] portion of the ramp** so the lowest band stays visible.

Transparency needs to be structured, not sprinkled. The basemap guidance is to let it recede: desaturate, grey out or lower its opacity ([Ordnance Survey, Effective basemaps](https://www.ordnancesurvey.co.uk/blog/effective-basemaps)); a worked example puts the imagery at 45% ([Caliper glossary](https://caliper.com/glossary/what-is-a-map-background.htm)) and a tutorial rule of thumb says 30–70% to see the basemap through an overlay ([Fused best practices](https://docs.fused.io/tutorials/Geospatial%20with%20Fused/best-practices)). Set the **minimap to 0.45–0.6 opacity or `filter: saturate(0.4)`**. Esri's warning is that transparency on overlapping polygons "compromises legibility because each color alters the other's appearance", and its fix is polygon outlines ([Esri ArcGIS Blog](https://esri.com/arcgis-blog/?p=121111)). The d3 output is nested, each threshold polygon being "the area where the input values are greater than or equal to the threshold value" ([d3-contour contours docs](https://d3js.org/d3-contour/contour)), so painting them in increasing order stacks five translucent layers on the hottest cell. Avoid the compounding by either painting the ">= t" polygons **opaque in increasing order inside one `<g opacity="0.5">`**, or emitting each band as a ring with the next level as a hole and `fill-rule="evenodd"`. Then add the **contour stroke, 0.5–1 px, `stroke-linejoin="round"`**, in the band color or white at 0.5 opacity, which doubles as the isoline layer and recovers the edges the fill blurs.

The legend follows the classed-map and hypsometric conventions together: a **vertical stack of 5 swatches, highest class on top**, each swatch rendered with the fill and opacity it actually has on the map over a neutral background and without outlines (the Esri hypsometric-legend rule is that patches show "the subdued colors as they appear on the map" and have no outlines ([Esri ArcGIS Blog, hypsometric legend](https://www.esri.com/arcgis-blog/products/arcgis-desktop/mapping/creating-a-legend-for-hypsometrically-tinted-shaded-relief))), labelled with the class range in the chosen unit. If the layer is drawn as a continuous ramp instead, the heat-map convention is a bar labelled only at the ends, "Sparse"/"Dense" in ArcGIS and "Minimum"/"Maximum" in QGIS ([ArcGIS Pro, Heat map symbology](https://doc.esri.com/en/arcgis-pro/latest/help/mapping/layer-properties/heat-map.html); [QgsHeatmapRenderer source](https://api.qgis.org/api/3.40/qgsheatmaprenderer_8cpp_source.html)). State in the caption when the breaks are per-game quantiles.

| Density layer parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Grid | native 150x150 cells (75x75 fallback); bilinear binning |
| Kernel | Gaussian sigma 7 cells (range 5–12) or quartic R = 20 cells; optional weight per death |
| Classes | 5 (max 7); breaks = quantiles of non-zero cells or geometric; lowest break ≈ 5% of peak |
| Ramp | single-hue light-to-dark, or viridis/cividis sampled over [0.15, 1.0] |
| Minimap | opacity 0.45–0.6 or `saturate(0.4)` |
| Bands | opaque fills, increasing order, one `<g opacity="0.45–0.6">`; stroke 0.5–1 px |
| Legend | vertical 5-swatch stack, high on top, no outlines, unit "deaths per 10x10 cells" or "% of peak" |

## Marching squares in 200 lines, or a rect mosaic in 20

Marching squares is the contouring algorithm in every one of the surveyed tools and is small enough to write directly. Threshold the field to bits ("Data equal to the isovalue has to be treated as above or below in a consistent way"), index each 2x2 block of samples by its four corner bits into 16 cases, emit the segments of a lookup table, place each vertex on the cell edge by linear interpolation, and resolve the two saddle cases with the average value at the cell center ([Wikipedia, Marching squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marching_squares)). The interpolation formula is `p = pa + (pb - pa) * (t - va) / (vb - va)`, d3's `smoothLinear` and Jamie Wong's `Q_y = B_y + (D_y - B_y) * (1 - f(B)) / (f(D) - f(B))` ([d3-contour src/contours.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/d3/d3-contour/main/src/contours.js); [Metaballs and Marching Squares](http://jamie-wong.com/2014/08/19/metaballs-and-marching-squares/)); without it the contours are visibly blocky, with it a 150-cell grid gives smooth bands at 448 px. d3 pads the grid with a below-threshold border and walks it in three passes so every ring closes, stitches fragments by matching endpoints, and classifies rings with positive signed area as exteriors and the rest as holes attached to the containing polygon ([d3-contour src/contours.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/d3/d3-contour/main/src/contours.js)). A workable table, with corners TL<<3 | TR<<2 | BR<<1 | BL and edges T, R, B, L, is: 1 L–B, 2 B–R, 3 L–R, 4 T–R, 6 T–B, 7 T–L, 8 T–L, 9 T–B, 11 T–R, 12 L–R, 13 R–B, 14 L–B, 0 and 15 nothing, and for saddles 5 and 10 use the center average c: case 5 gives T–L and R–B when c >= t, else T–R and L–B; case 10 gives T–R and L–B when c >= t, else T–L and R–B. That table is derived, not quoted (the sources show theirs as images, and Wong's tutorial is internally inconsistent about bit order), so **fix one convention and unit-test it on a single Gaussian blob** before trusting it. Each threshold's MultiPolygon becomes one `<path d="M ... Z M ... Z">`, so a 5-class map is 5 paths for fills plus 5 for strokes.

The simpler alternative is to skip contouring: classify the KDE grid and draw one `<rect>` per cell or per block in the class color, which is the raster model Datashader (aggregate, `spread` 1–3 px, `shade`) and the QGIS heatmap renderer (per-pixel kernel sum, normalized, ramp lookup) both use ([Datashader API](https://datashader.org/api.html); [QgsHeatmapRenderer source](https://api.qgis.org/api/3.40/qgsheatmaprenderer_8cpp_source.html)). At 3 px per cell the staircase is at pixel scale and invisible; at a 50x50 block grid (9 px squares) it reads as a deliberate mosaic. Every cell is painted once, so there is no stacked transparency to manage, and `shape-rendering="crispEdges"` removes seams. The cost is a heavy DOM (22,500 rects at full resolution) unless horizontal runs of equal class are merged into single rects or the grid is coarsened to 75x75 (5,625) or 50x50 (2,500), and the loss of isolines. Hexbins are not worth it: they count rather than smooth, and with a few hundred points most 8–12 px hexes hold 0–3 deaths, so the result is noisy unless smoothed first, at which point the rect grid is equivalent and simpler.

## Color fights by time, ring them, paint the newest last

Time is ordered, so the engagement map treats it like density: a sequential ramp, never a categorical palette or a rainbow. Datawrapper's rule is that sequential scales "go from bright to dark or the other way round" for values that run low to high, while hue palettes are for categories "that don't have an intrinsic order" ([Datawrapper, Which color scale](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/which-color-scale-to-use-in-data-vis)). The GIS implementation of exactly this is Esri's "Continuous Timeline (color)" and "Age (color)" styles, a single date field mapped to a sequential ramp that can be inverted so either newest or oldest is darkest ([Esri, Style time](https://doc.esri.com/en/arcgis-enterprise/latest/create/style-time-mv.html)), and the older Tracking Analyst divides a time window into N equal classes ("a time window size of 60 seconds and six classes, each class spans 10 seconds") ([Esri Tracking Analyst](https://desktop.arcgis.com/en/arcmap/latest/extensions/tracking-analyst/060-symbolizing-point-data-by-color.htm)). The direction is a stated choice, not a rule; for a post-game review make **the latest fights the darkest and most saturated**, because they are also painted on top and the reader asks "how did it end". Class the ramp at **round game-time boundaries (0–5, 5–10, ... min), 5 classes under 20 fights and 6–7 for 20–50**, within the 3–7 range cartography textbooks give ([MapColorAI, arXiv 2503.15502](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.15502); [SAGE GIScience, Classification](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/geoinfoscience/chpt/classification-data)); a continuous ramp labelled at the two ends also works because hover recovers exact timestamps. Clamp the ramp to roughly its 15–90% lightness range so the earliest fights do not vanish into a light basemap. Prefer a single hue (light amber to dark brown, or light to dark blue) if the minimap is colorful and the events should read as one layer; use plasma, viridis or batlow when 7 steps must stay distinguishable ([Crameri et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7595127/)).

Overlap in space but not in time is the central problem, and the sources agree on the failure and the fix. Symbol-level transparency "will also blend the colors of transparent symbols that overlap, resulting in symbology that is not in your legend" ([Esri ArcWatch](https://www.esri.com/about/newsroom/arcwatch/learn-how-to-choose-the-right-transparency-option-for-your-web-map), via search summary, the page refused the fetch); translucent dots show density but make "individual dots very faint", and at some density "the individual points coalesce into a solid mass" ([Wikipedia, Dot distribution map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot_distribution_map)). The historical precedents handle it by keeping identities apart: Snow stacked cases per house rather than overlapping them ([Wikipedia, 1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1854_Broad_Street_cholera_outbreak)), Shapter's 1849 Exeter map used "different point symbols to represent the cases in each of the three years" ([Wikipedia, Dot distribution map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot_distribution_map)), and Minard drew the advance and the retreat as two side-by-side bands, "the red designates the men who enter Russia, the black those who leave it" ([Wikipedia, Minard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Joseph_Minard)). The static translation: **fill-opacity 0.3 per disc (0.25 above 25 fights, 0.4 below 10), a 1.25 px stroke of the same time color at opacity 0.9, 2 px for the most recent 10–20% of fights, sorted by start time so the newest is painted last**. Three stacked 0.3 fills reach 1 − 0.7³ = 0.66, so the minimap still shows through and the top ring still reads; beyond 4–5 stacked discs the fills saturate, which is acceptable because it signals a hotspot and hover lists the fights. The reader reads time from the ring and intensity from the fill, and the legend promises only that. If the ramp is single-hue, `mix-blend-mode: multiply` keeps overlaps darkening without hue shifts; with a multi-hue ramp keep normal alpha, since multiplying yellow by purple gives an unreadable brown. For two fights sharing a center, offset the second by 2–4 px or vary the stroke (solid/dashed), Shapter's trick in SVG.

Radius should encode magnitude as a proportional symbol does: **r ∝ sqrt(units or supply lost), clamped to 4–28 px at 28rem** so the largest disc is about a sixteenth of the map width and the smallest is still a hit target. Hover is the "slider lite" that makes this static layer analyzable without a scrubber: thicken the hovered ring to 3 px and its fill to 0.6, dim the others to fill-opacity 0.1, and highlight the matching timeline tick and list row. The newsroom convention for few events, direct labels instead of a key ("You might be able to use direct labels instead" ([Datawrapper, Color keys](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/color-keys-for-data-visualizations))), fits 5–50 fights: **number each ring with a small white-on-dark numeral and list the fights in time order beside the map** with timestamp and summary. This survives both alpha blending and small size.

Animation is the one tempting option the evidence rejects for the default view. In the InfoVis test-of-time study, "animation is the least effective form for analysis; both static depictions of trends are significantly faster than animation, and the small multiples display is more accurate" ([Robertson et al. 2008](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~stasko/papers/infovis08-anim.pdf)). Live conflict maps avoid color-for-time altogether and rely on date filters (Liveuamap's date picker, ACLED's date filter, a timeline bar whose clicked day filters the map ([Google Maps Mania 2014](https://googlemapsmania.blogspot.com/2014/07/mapping-crisis-in-ukraine.html); [ACLED Ukraine Conflict Monitor](https://acleddata.com/ukraine-conflict-monitor/); [ConflictWatch Live](https://conflictwatch-live.vercel.app/pages/how-to-use.html))), and game tools such as ggViz scrub with a frame slider ([ggViz, arXiv 2107.06495](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2107.06495)). Offer a scrubber as an extra if replay is wanted, and 2–4 small multiples (early/mid/late game) as a toggle, each about 9rem wide at this size, but make the static ringed map the default.

| Engagement layer parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Time encoding | sequential ramp, newest darkest; 5 classes (<20 fights) or 6–7 (20–50) at round minutes; or continuous with end labels |
| Ramp | single hue light-to-dark, or plasma/viridis/batlow, clamped to 15–90% lightness |
| Disc | r ∝ sqrt(magnitude), 4–28 px; fill-opacity 0.3 (0.25 >25 fights, 0.4 <10) |
| Ring | same color, opacity 0.9, 1.25 px; 2 px for most recent 10–20%; draw in time order |
| Blending | `multiply` for single-hue ramp, normal alpha for multi-hue |
| Labels | numeral per fight + time-ordered list; 2–4 px offset for shared centers |
| Hover | hovered ring 3 px / fill 0.6, others fill 0.1; sync with timeline strip and list |

## Legends state the unit, the direction and the per-game breaks

Both layers need their legends to show the symbol as it actually appears, which is the single convention the density and event sources share. For the time layer, a **10–12rem horizontal gradient or 5–7 swatch bar under the map, labelled "0:00" at the left and the game length at the right with one or two ticks at round minutes**, titled with the variable ("When the fight happened"). Datawrapper's rules: "label the min and max values", "only label visibly distinct colors", "in static visualizations, err on the side of showing more values in a color key", and an arrow or histogram beside the key helps reading ([Datawrapper, Color keys](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/color-keys-for-data-visualizations)). Draw the swatch as the map symbol, a ring in the class color over a light fill, so nobody expects solid discs. If radius encodes magnitude, add a 2–3 circle size key ("10 / 50 / 100 supply"). A **one-dimensional timeline strip under the map in the same ramp, one tick per fight**, doubles as the legend and exposes the temporal clustering the map hides; Minard's dated temperature chart along the bottom is the ancestor ("the temperature shown across the bottom supports interpretation" ([ICA Map Design, Minard](https://mapdesign.icaci.org/?p=2170))). For the density layer, the vertical swatch stack described above, and a one-line caption naming the unit and whether breaks are fixed or per game. Esri's Tracking Analyst legend table (Symbol, Range, Label, with free-text labels) is the model for the classed variant ([Esri Tracking Analyst](https://desktop.arcgis.com/en/arcmap/latest/extensions/tracking-analyst/060-symbolizing-point-data-by-color.htm)).

## Rainbow ramps, compounding alpha and twenty thresholds are the traps

The pitfalls cluster into color, transparency and classification errors, and most of them are the defaults of some popular tool. Rainbow and jet ramps top the list: they fail "to reproduce a meaningful smooth gradient", their yellow "is the brightest colour and attracts the eye the most", and color-vision deficiency affects "0.5% of women and 8% of men" ([Crameri et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7595127/)); in a hydrology journal 47% of 2020 publications still had a rainbow or red-green problem ([Stoelzle & Stein 2021](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/25/4549/2021/)). On a game map a rainbow is doubly wrong because the terrain already uses many hues and the yellow band would falsely emphasize the mid-game. The hypsometric green-brown-white palette is a cartographically literate but wrong choice over terrain for the land-cover misreading documented above. Red-green pairings as team colors against a density ramp are a related trap; keep team identity on strokes or icons, not on the sequential ramp.

Transparency errors come next. Per-path `fill-opacity` on nested contour bands compounds five layers deep on the hottest cell; put the opacity on the group. Per-disc alpha on engagements produces blended colors "not in your legend" ([Esri ArcWatch](https://www.esri.com/about/newsroom/arcwatch/learn-how-to-choose-the-right-transparency-option-for-your-web-map), via search summary); the ring and the draw order carry the identity. A fully opaque or very dark basemap defeats both layers; a basemap at full saturation competes with them. No peer-reviewed source fixes the opacity numbers, so the 0.45–0.6 (basemap and bands) and 0.25–0.4 (discs) values are vendor rules of thumb to tune by eye, not standards.

Classification errors are the quiet ones. More than 7 classes cannot be matched to a legend ([ArcGIS Pro, Graduated colors](https://doc.esri.com/en/arcgis-pro/latest/help/mapping/layer-properties/graduated-colors.html)); d3's 20 default thresholds are for unlabelled smooth stacks. Equal-interval breaks on a skewed density put four of five classes into a few cells ([Axis Maps, Choropleth guide](https://www.axismaps.com/guide/choropleth)). A lowest threshold of zero paints the whole map. Per-game quantile breaks silently change meaning between two games placed side by side. A too-small bandwidth (under ~5 cells) shows individual deaths as pimples, a too-large one (over ~12) merges separate battles. On the time layer, quantile class boundaries give unreadable labels like "7:43–12:18"; use round minutes. Animation as the only view trades accuracy for excitement ([Robertson et al. 2008](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~stasko/papers/infovis08-anim.pdf)). A Minard-style path linking fights in time order tangles with 5–50 fights at several bases; reserve it for one army's path or for the hovered sequence. In the code, the marching-squares bit order must be fixed once and tested, and ties at the isovalue treated consistently as inside.

## Conclusion

The two layers are the same visualization problem seen twice: an ordered scalar (density, time) over a basemap that must recede, encoded in lightness, cut into 5 classes, outlined so transparency never has to carry identity. That symmetry is worth exploiting in the implementation: one sequential-ramp sampler, one legend component parameterized by orientation and unit, one group-opacity convention, and the same 28rem coordinate transform from the 150-cell grid. The unexpected finding is how little the tools disagree once their pixel-based defaults are converted to map cells; the d3, ArcGIS and Leaflet defaults all land on a 6–8 cell kernel and a sub-2-cell grid for this target, so the only real design decision is how far below the Scott/Silverman bandwidth to go for a multimodal battlefield.

What the research does not settle is empirical: no study measures how badly readers misjudge the time of a fight under alpha blending, nor fixes an opacity for overlays, nor tests time-colored event maps at sidebar widths. Those gaps are exactly what a replay viewer with hover and a tunable bandwidth slider can answer for its own users; ship the defaults above, expose sigma and class count, and watch which settings people keep.
